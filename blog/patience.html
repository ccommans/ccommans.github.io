Patience and Rejection

by Colin
May 17 2026

And seekest thou great things for thyself? Seek them not.
— Jeremiah 45:5 KJV

If you want to make God laugh, tell Him your plans.
— Woody Allen

If today I received from God everything that I had prayed and hoped for yesterday, I would be ecstatic; everything that I had hoped for a month ago, I would be happy; hopes from six months ago, content; a year ago, unsure; 4 years ago, in tears. And although the conclusion from this meditation is cliché—God cannot have been the one to have changed, but rather myself—against better judgement, I still would say that actualizing my current hopes would make my life better, i.e. right now I know what is best for me. Of course this radical stubbornness is, quite frankly, my immediate response to simply not getting what I wanted, but there is also a genuine belief that I have grown wiser over time through the experiences and consequences of my previous choices and so I am better-informed not only in my current decisions but also in my future ones. Therefore being rejected and necessarily having to be patient in response stings all the more because it means this mindset is not yet calibrated to the timing of God.

The word "patience" in the Bible (e.g. love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, etc. in Galatians 5) is the common translation for the Greek makrothymia, which literally means long-tempered or being slow to anger; the linguistic imagery is that of an explosive with an extrordinarily long fuse. In this sense, when translating makrothymia I prefer versions like the KJV which use the word "longsuffering", because that is a much more honest expression of the experience I have when I am being meaningfully tested. When I am waiting in line at the grocery store, I am patient; when I am waiting in life in singleness, I am longsuffering.

Looking at the patience-with-singleness facet specifically, why is it such a personal slight when feelings are not mutual or when the other person is already seeing someone else? Why is Darcy fleeing the town in Pride and Prejudice is a completely understandable response to Elizabeth's rejection? The only possible reason I can think of is that there is so much embarrassment when a personal pursuit, even when done in completely good faith, ends up completely backfiring; the worst part about eating crow is that it is in public. Sometimes I hear stories where people say something like "God granted me my prideful, shortsighted desires to be with this person in order to show me why that was not going to be best for me long-term and for His will"—fantastic, I wish God would let me learn such a lesson in that way too instead of facing rejections[1]; as the KJV puts it, "why castest thou off my soul?"[2]

The imagery of seeds are particularly comforting to me in times of agitated impatience or discontentment, I think because (a) waiting and having patience is simply an unavoidable aspect of agriculture and (b) it is a reminder of the possibility of complete transformation of seed to flower, of heartbreak to celebration. For example, Psalm 126 says that those who sow with tears will reap with songs of joy. My (possibly all-time) favorite teaching from Jesus is found in Mark and also concerns a growing seed:

He also said, "This is what the Kingdom of God is like. A man scatters seed on the ground. Night and day, whether he sleeps or gets up, the seed sprouts and grows, though he does not know how. All by itself the soil produces grain—first the stalk, then the head, then the full kernel in the head. As soon as the grain is ripe, he puts the sickle to it, because the harvest has come."
— Mark 4:26–29 NIV

I love this passage because it really resists the typical framework we often apply of perfectly matching up one thing in the parable with exactly one thing in real life. In fact, the parable immediately before this is the more-famous one about seeds growing or dying depending on their environment and is given this one-to-one interpretation by Jesus himself. However, the parable here cannot be analyzed in this same single-dimension method. It is not an explicit commandment for us to just be the farmer and sow seeds in faith with a future harvest in mind (i.e. "believe and see"), since it misses out on the message that we ourselves are growing. Nor is it saying to just be the seed which God the farmer will grow us in unpredictable fashion (i.e. "God works in mysterious ways"), since narratively the farmer is clueless and uninvolved with the seed's growth. The problem is that neither interpretation offers the complete modus operandi of the Kingdom of God[3], both lack the proper inclusion of the mystery of real life. The resolution of this is understanding that Jesus paradoxically asks us as Christians to see ourselves as both the farmer and the seed while going through life, and only with this new dual perspective can we actually gain a framework to properly handle difficulties.

It is incredibly easy to ruin or forget this farmer/seed duality, since it is by nature paradoxical and human categories of thought cannot hold the two together well. Conventional secular wisdom says that we are all farmers who reap what we sow, so we should not be idle but rather plan ahead and be opportunistic and strategic etc. etc. so that we may see the fruits of our labor, and then life will go better. Conventional religious[4] wisdom says that we are just seeds that ultimately have no final say in our growth, since God controls absolutely everything, even the very smallest things, so only if we have absolute trust in God will life go better. To further add to the debate, both of these viewpoints are actually considered wise in the Bible:

Secular / Farmer Religious / Seed
"Good planning and hard work lead to prosperity, but hasty shortcuts lead to poverty." — Proverbs 21:5 NLT "You can make many plans, but the LORD’s purpose will prevail." — Proverbs 19:21 NLT

So which viewpoint should we choose? Both work quite well when things are all going according to plan, yet both completely fall apart when met with obstacle: the farmer alone gets totally overwhelmed because his future is completely determined by his choices, so he tries to whip up even more work, and the seed alone gets too cynical and self-flagellating trying to whip up even more faith. With this parable Jesus offers a lifeline: our choices are undoubtedly our own, and yet we can relax because He will be in control of it all and not abandon us, even though we don't deserve it. It is only by the grace of God that both the seeds we plant can grow into fruit which supports us, as well as the work God does within us to grow closer to Christ in relationship and character. Hopefully the cycle which results from this is clear: faithful deeds and recognizing the fruit God allows to cultivate from them align us closer to God's purpose, making our future deeds all the more faithful to God's pre-existing purpose, which then produces more fruit, etc.—Jesus is saying this is a fundamental way the Kingdom of God operates and is completely revolutionary to the old thinking of the world.

Furthermore, note that the parable also tells us we will not get the intimate knowledge about how this whole Kingdom cycle actually goes about happening, nor the ability to control when things happen—all we know is that God is always at work producing evidence of his own goodness; again to use the KJV for verse 28, "for the earth bringeth forth fruit of herself." It is in this sense of unconditionally forgoing complete understanding that we are called to radically trust in God and have makrothymia. And of course what is so radical about this trust is that it must be present even in difficulty; gems are not polished without friction. This precisely is the thrust behind Peter's description of the Christian life in 1 Peter 4 as purosis—a refining of metal which can only occur when the entire ore is placed in fire to burn off the impurities. Peter guarentees that every Christian goes through such a process: it is optional in the sense that the Christian chose to follow Jesus (i.e. is a farmer), but once opted-in, the trials of life become an all-consuming fire that radically change you as a person at the mercy of God (i.e. is a seed). Such an understanding is another reason for my preference of the use of "longsuffering" to talk about this radical trust; "patience" seems to wrap up everything too nicely compared to the fiery testings of life which the biblical authors so commonly talk about.

So yes, I do wish I did not have to face rejection in life, because it is really hard. Specifically, it is hard sitting in the sadness yet believing that somehow, someway everything is going to be okay even though every natural instinct I have fights its hardest to convince me otherwise; the spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak. An exhortation comes later in 1 Peter 4: rejection and suffering actually makes me partners with Jesus since He too suffered—it is fellowship by commiseration in the literal sense. At this point in my life, this idea has had the best success against these natural instincts that worry about my future. I agree with Tim Keller[5] in believing that there is not exactly one pre-ordained path for me in my life that I will have to keep fumbling around until I find it or God shows me it (or some combination of the two). Otherwise I feel there is too much pressure to make plans for my future that figure out the specific job, wife, home, etc. which God already chose for me. Instead, there is a generally right way of living which is discerned from this radically-trusting makrothymia that gives me confidence that the places I choose to work, people I pursue to date, where I decide to live, etc. will contribute to advancing the will of God in my life and the lives of those around me. sI like to think that the plans I have today are not too far off this right way of living. Yet even if I fail or face rejection along the way, God is preparing me for something I cannot currently envision.

Commit your work to the LORD, and your plans will be established.
— Proverbs 16:3 ESV

Unconditionally trust God (via your works, not plans!)—make a radically unconditional trust commitment with Him—and you will slowly become the kind of person whose plans (not works!) are wise and makes good choices.
— Proverbs 16:3 Tim Keller paraphrase

We give ourselves too much credit.
