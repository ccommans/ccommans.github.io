Answered Prayers

by Colin
May 21 2026

And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good
— Romans 8:28 ESV

I recently heard somebody saying that they were pulling into a parking lot, and the space closest to the building opened up, and they said it was a sign that God was with them.
— Rob Bell

When I was playing sports as a kid, sometimes both my team and the other team would pray in our own separate pregame huddles, and naturally in our huddle whoever was praying would (usually sneakily) ask God for our team to win. What crossed my mind later was the question what if the other team also prayed for a victory? Surely God could not have both of us win, so what were the factors that God was weighing when He chose a team to win the game? Was it the more devout team? The more generous team? The more Christ-like team? In retrospect, more often than not the team that won was the simply more athletic and better-prepared one.

The example is—in the most literal sense—childish, but for the longest time this general idea of asking God, who then would weigh certain things to determine His response, was how I perceived the petition aspect of prayer. Jesus seems to be pretty clear in Matthew 7:8 that "for every one that asketh receiveth" yet Job seems to better capture reality when he grieves the fact God is not answering his request to face and talk to Him while also observing

Why do the wicked live on, growing old and increasing in power?...Yet they say to God, "Leave us alone! We have no desire to know your ways. Who is the Almighty, that we should serve him? What would we gain by praying to him?"
— Job 21:7, 14–15 NIV

So what is going on? How do we harmonize these two conflicting messages? There are direct answers:

The "good things" which God will surely give do not necessarily include everything that his children might like to have. The carte blanche approach to petitionary prayer does not find support from the NT as a whole. It is God as the Father in heaven who knows what is "good" for his children, and as with a human parent his generosity may not always coincide with the child's wishes. But for all that necessary caution, there is an openness about [Matthew 7:7–8] which invites not merely a resigned acceptance of what the Father gives, but a willingness to explore the extent of his generosity, secure in the knowledge that only what is "good" will be given, so that mistakes in prayer through human short-sightedness will not rebound on those praying. There is, fortunately, nothing inevitable or mechanical about God's answers to his people's requests...The childlike confidence of vv. 7–8 is the prerogative only of disciples who...have a true relationship with their Father in heaven.
— R.T. France, The Gospel of Matthew p. 279

which, although intellectually satisfying, still creates more questions to be answered when processed through our personal experiences with prayer, particularly the notion that whatever we receive in response is to be considered good. Prayer is fundamentally about being in communication with God, and both instinctually and with the encouragement of Jesus we use this line of communication to ask God what we want. Sometimes we pray for things that really only would directly affect ourselves (e.g. recovery in sickness, peace of mind). Sometimes we pray for things that indirectly affect others (e.g. discernment for a career decision while having a family to provide for, how to spend or save money earned). And sometimes we pray for things that directly involve someone else (e.g. success in pursuing someone romantically, that some recently-met stranger may find Christ). It is this third scenario that I want to meditate on, which hopefully can begin some generalization, possibly then changing the understanding of the first two scenarios—and then ultimately of prayer itself.

When Bob prays for something directly involving someone else, let's call her Alice, he is necessarily putting his wants above hers[1]. This, by the way, is not a bad thing a priori, but it must be recognized. As a society, we allow the parent to have say over the wants of their children (as of recent, less so...), a boss over their employees, a teacher over their students, etc. etc. But these examples are all in some existing hierarchy, what about when Alice and Bob are simply contemporaries and equal in standing vis-à-vis the context of Bob's prayer? Put another way, what about when Alice has no reason to think that her wants may conflict with Bob's?

Let's get more specific: Bob plans to ask out Alice, as he has been diligently praying for clarity on whether he should pursue her and ultimately prays that she says yes. However, Alice is, unbeknownst to Bob, already dating Charlie which she then breaks the news to Bob about. Therefore, Bob's prayer is not answered, i.e. is answered but negatively[2], and he feels dejected, embarrassed, frustrated, etc. What, then, should be Bob's takeaway from this experience? We would probably tell him sorry, it just wasn't in the cards, there are plenty of other fish in the sea, and so on. But in terms of his relationship to prayer, the tough pill to swallow is that sometimes God simply says no.

But yet further unbeknownst to Bob, before any of this, Alice has been praying to meet a man of God to date, and Charlie is actually the answer to Alice's prayers. Rather suddenly the entire situation has been flipped on its head, and in our reconstruction we must not forget that this probably changes our thoughts about Bob's original prayer. On the surface, Bob was just too late, and a whole investigation can be done into how we understand and try to accept the timing of God about these things (which will not be done here). But maybe more important, does this new information change Bob's attitude about his prayer—or maybe even more importantly, should it? I would argue that it depends on where Bob is in his faith and his emotional maturity and his perception of Alice as a sister in Christ with her own desires, wants, hopes, etc.

Your heartbreak could actually be the result of another person's prayer being answered, and so your response to God could in a sense also be indicative of your thoughts about that person. I don't mean this by the way in a moralizing sense, it is just an observation with a context-dependent interpretation. More generally, when we pray for something to happen which involves someone else, we must understand that the other person also may have prayers of their own. And again, the conclusion is not that we should therefore never make petitionary prayers and live extremely passively so as to not step on anyone's toes, but rather when we have this fact in mind, it should probably make such prayers sound different. This undoubtedly adds some tension to prayer life, but then again there are many instances of prayer in the Bible which cannot hide such tension; Jesus's most emotional prayer comes to mind:

Then Jesus went with his disciples to a place called Gethsemane, and he said to them, "Sit here while I go over there and pray." He took Peter and the two sons of Zebedee along with him, and he began to be sorrowful and troubled. Then he said to them, "My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death. Stay here and keep watch with me." Going a little farther, he fell with his face to the ground and prayed, "My Father, if it is possible, may this cup be taken from me. Yet not as I will, but as you will."
— Matthew 26:36–39 NIV

Ultimately what is relevant here to the discussion is that within the prayer there is 1) a conflict of will and 2) a conscious choice of one will over the other. Speaking only for myself but probably not alone, neither of these are often explicitly found in my prayers, but could definitely be included if I were to be a little more honest about my requests. I am more often than not asking for something that will impact more than my own life; no man is an island. Choosing God's will above the one praying certainly makes its way into the Lord's Prayer, though over time the formulaic nature of the historic prayer has relegated that aspect to a more resigned acceptance rather than the honest wrestling found in the Gethesemane prayer. Regardless, petitionary prayers take on almost a completely fresh form when we include these new variables, and Paul seemed to understand this quite intimately:

Rejoice in the Lord always; again I will say, rejoice. Let your reasonableness be known to everyone. The Lord is at hand; do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus.
— Philippians 4:4–7 ESV

and typical of his writing style, he gives us so much to unpack even in a simple exhortation. In particular the phrase "supplication with thanksgiving" is an important, if not the key, idea about prayer which Paul is trying to express. What makes it so important is that it is actually oxymoronic; many translations add commas (in my view, mistakenly) to try to rectify a more sensible idea of petition and gratitude being separate components of a prayer (cf. the common A.C.T.S. acronym for prayer structure). But taking this grammatically minimalist approach instead argues for the ability to supplicate with thanksgiving without divorcing the two; it is all done in one fluid motion. The coup de grâce against this is the question of how can I be thankful to God about the request I just made? Thankfulness is always a response, not an anticipation. Even a general sense of thankfulness only necessitates being thankful once the events actually happen. And yet as Paul continues he effectively doubles down, saying that practicing this in prayer actually gives you a capital-P Peace—eirene tou Theou in the Greek—which by definition will supersede any possible human understanding, and hence also any human misunderstanding. Also note that the intent of my translation of eirene tou Theou as simply "Peace" is not to endorse a transcendentalist "peace and love" viewpoint or pantheistically remove God from scripture, but rather to best capture the intent of Paul talking about a groundbreaking peace-above-all-peaces and—at least in my mind—the phrase "peace of God" gets too entangled with "peace with God" (i.e. equal standing), "peace from God" (i.e. lack of wrath), "peace in God" (i.e. confidence), etc.

In a sense, thanking God for the request you make means it was never about the request in the first place. Requesting something comes from a perceived want, which after abstracting a little means that there is a perceiver—who can then be changed for the better in the eyes of God. That might result in directly responding to that perceived want, but Paul is saying that it also may result in circumstances which utterly baffle you; even in the extreme case of Job's suffering we see this theoretical absurdism actually in practice as he says "though he slay me, yet will I trust in him" (Job 13:15). In either case, supplication with thanksgiving is how this sort of perspective and proper expectation can even be achieved. This is what Kierkegaard gets at when he says "The function of prayer is not to influence God, but rather to change the nature of the one who prays."[3] It is more than simply aligning our will to the will of God—how on earth would we even be able to handle a fraction of it?—but it is equally not just sending a list to Santa Claus with the hope that our requests appear under the tree one morning. Both perspectives are too one-sided (God gives all He wants to me vs. I give all I want to God) to form a meaningful relationship—which is admittedly cliché but to counter with another one, finding the plan of God not only acceptable but beautiful along the journey of relational reconciliation with Him through the sacrifice of Christ is the whole point.

Never have a bad attitude about life, which should be the norm as a Christian, and make it known to those around you. God is with you and wants to hear from you, so you have nothing to worry about; and when you do talk to Him, ask Him for what is on your mind and in the same breath thank Him for His response. This may not make sense to you, but this is how this proper attitude and perspective, which is the perspective Christ holds, called Peace can also be held by you and get you through anything in life.
— Philippians 4:4–7, my paraphrase

This is by no means a perfect or comprehensive meditation on prayer—just off the top of my head I have failed to mention the Holy Spirit as intercessor, the role of the presence of God, and the confession of sins. As Christians we are called to pray without ceasing, but yet prayer is extraordinarily difficult for me to even wrap my head around when I try to put it into practice, let alone doing it continually. I have notions and vague ideas and really try to resist just going through the motions, and I wish I could have recorded every detail of my internal state and external surroundings the few times I have actually experienced that capital-P Peace so that I could recreate it when later troubles came my way, but I think the mystery of it all is simply a baked-in aspect. And maybe that is a pat answer which will evolve into something more concrete as I grow older, which I hope is true, but it remains a hope nevertheless in the meantime. And the temptation is to throw out everything just because I haven't fully understood this and succumb to the all-too-human frustration of not seeing my prayers be answered. But I haven’t lost hope. Nor love. Nor joy. Nor truth. Nor compassion. Nor generosity. Nor my relationship with the living God.
